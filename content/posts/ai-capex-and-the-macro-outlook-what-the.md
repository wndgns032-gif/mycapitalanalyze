At the Forbes Global CEO Conference 2026 in Singapore, the heads of two AI application unicorns pushed back on the idea that artificial intelligence is mostly hype. Their argument rests on commercial demand rather than venture funding or media attention. For macro watchers, that claim matters less as a technology story than as a signal about capital spending, productivity, and the policy response that follows.

## Key Facts

- The panel took place at the Forbes Global CEO Conference 2026 in Singapore.
- Leaders of two AI application unicorns said strong commercial demand shows AI is more than hype.
- The discussion also covered regulation and security risks.
- No financial figures or forecasts were disclosed.

The executives did not present revenue numbers or market projections. Their case was qualitative: customers are paying for AI tools, which they treat as evidence of durable demand rather than a speculative bubble. They also acknowledged that regulation and security concerns are live issues for the sector.

## Analysis

For macroeconomists, the interesting question is not whether AI is real but whether it is large enough to move aggregate demand and productivity. Business investment in computing and software is a modest share of GDP, but it is one of the more cyclical components. If AI application vendors are seeing genuine paid demand, that supports the case for continued capex in data centers, chips, and enterprise software. That spending shows up in GDP as gross private domestic investment and can lift near-term growth even before productivity gains arrive.

The productivity channel is slower and harder to measure. General-purpose technologies typically take years to diffuse through firms, and the gains often appear in specific tasks rather than headline output. Executives at an application layer, selling tools to other businesses, are well placed to observe adoption, but their view is partial. They see paying customers, not the full economy.

The mention of regulation and security risks is also macro-relevant. Compliance costs and data-security requirements can raise barriers to entry and favor larger vendors, which affects market concentration and pricing power. For central banks, AI adoption cuts both ways: it could lift supply capacity and ease inflation over time, but the investment boom itself adds to demand in the short run.

## Implications

- **Capex watch:** Treat AI-related investment as a support for growth, but monitor whether orders at chipmakers and cloud providers keep pace with the narrative.
- **Productivity patience:** Do not price in rapid economy-wide productivity gains. Diffusion takes time, and measured output may lag anecdotal adoption.
- **Regulatory risk:** Security and compliance rules could reshape competitive dynamics, favoring incumbents with scale.
- **Market concentration:** If AI spending concentrates among a few large firms, equity indices become more sensitive to their capital plans.
- **Inflation mix:** Near-term demand from the investment boom may offset some disinflationary supply effects.

For investors, the practical takeaway is to separate the technology story from the earnings story. Commercial demand at the application layer is a positive signal, but it does not by itself justify valuations across the entire supply chain. The macro backdrop — interest rates, capex cycles, and regulation — will determine how much of the AI narrative converts into durable cash flow.

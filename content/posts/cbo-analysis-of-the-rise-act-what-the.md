The Congressional Budget Office has released a cost estimate for S. 3589, the RISE Act, a higher-education measure that the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions advanced on August 5, 2026. While the headline number may be modest relative to the multi-trillion-dollar debates that dominate fiscal coverage, the estimate matters because it touches one of the most politically sensitive and economically consequential corners of the federal budget: student aid.

## Key Facts

- The legislation is known as the RISE Act and carries the Senate bill number S. 3589.
- It was reported by the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions on August 5, 2026.
- The Congressional Budget Office, the legislature's nonpartisan fiscal scorekeeper, produced the cost estimate.
- The estimate is the official accounting of how the bill would affect federal spending, revenues, and deficits over the budget window.
- As a committee-reported bill, it still faces the full Senate legislative process before any provisions could become law.

## Analysis

Budget scorekeeping is often dismissed as inside-baseball, but it is the mechanism that determines whether a bill can move. Under current Senate rules, legislation that increases the deficit beyond the budget window can be vulnerable to procedural challenges, which is why a CBO score is not merely informational — it is a gatekeeping document. For the RISE Act, the score tells lawmakers, lobbyists, and investors how much fiscal room the proposal consumes and whether it can be paired with offsets.

Higher-education spending sits at the intersection of several macro forces. Federal student aid influences enrollment, tuition pricing, and household balance sheets. It also flows into the broader economy through university spending, research grants, and the labor supply of graduates. When aid rules change, the effects ripple through sectors such as education services, student housing, and consumer credit. That is why even a bill that looks narrow can carry wider market significance.

From a macro perspective, the key question is whether the bill is deficit-neutral, deficit-increasing, or deficit-reducing. If the CBO estimate shows a net cost, it adds to an already strained fiscal trajectory at a time when debt-service costs are absorbing a growing share of federal revenue. If it shows savings or is fully offset, it becomes easier to advance and less likely to complicate broader budget negotiations. Either way, the score shapes the bargaining positions of senators who care about fiscal discipline and those who prioritize expanding access to education.

## Implications

For investors, the direct market impact of a single committee-reported education bill is limited. The more relevant signal is what the score reveals about the direction of federal spending and the appetite for new programs. A pattern of deficit-financed expansions would reinforce the case for higher term premiums in Treasuries over time, while a commitment to offsets would be modestly disinflationary in fiscal terms.

For policy watchers, the RISE Act is a test case. It shows whether Congress can still pass meaningful education legislation in a polarized environment, and whether the pay-for debate can be resolved without derailing the bill. The CBO estimate is the first hard data point in that process, and it will be cited by both supporters and opponents.

For households and students, the practical stakes are straightforward: eligibility rules, grant amounts, and loan terms can shift the cost of higher education and the burden of repayment. Those changes influence decisions about enrollment, borrowing, and career paths, with long-run effects on productivity and earnings.

## FAQ

### What is the RISE Act?
It is a Senate higher-education bill, designated S. 3589, that was reported by the Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor and Pensions on August 5, 2026. The Congressional Budget Office has now issued its cost estimate for the proposal.

### Why does a CBO score matter for markets?
The score quantifies the bill's fiscal impact, which determines whether it can advance under budget rules and whether it adds to federal borrowing needs. That, in turn, feeds into expectations about deficits, Treasury issuance, and interest rates.

### Does the bill become law automatically?
No. Being reported out of committee is an early step. The bill must still pass the full Senate, reconcile with any House version, and be signed by the president before any of its provisions take effect.

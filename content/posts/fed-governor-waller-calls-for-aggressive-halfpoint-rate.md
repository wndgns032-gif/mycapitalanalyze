The Federal Reserve is facing renewed pressure to act decisively as price growth shows signs of re‑acceleration. In a live interview, Governor Christopher Waller warned that a modest 0.5 percentage‑point hike at the December policy meeting could be essential to "shock" markets and bring inflation back on a downward path.

## Key Facts
- Waller indicated that a 50‑basis‑point increase at the Dec. 8‑9 FOMC meeting is likely required to counter the latest inflation uptick.
- He suggested an alternative path of a 25‑basis‑point move now, followed by a clear signal of another hike in January, though this diverges from Chair Kevin Warsh’s preferred communication style.
- Recent CPI data for October showed consumer prices rising faster than most forecasts, pushing headline inflation above the 3 % range.
- The market’s FedWatch gauge currently prices in a 25‑basis‑point hike in December, with expectations of at least one more increase before the end of 2027.
- Other Fed officials are split: St. Louis President Musalem sees up to four hikes by spring 2027, while New York President John Williams favors a more measured approach.
- The central bank previously reversed three 0.75 percentage‑point cuts made in 2025, and Waller argues those cuts now need to be undone.

## Analysis
The prospect of a half‑point hike signals a shift back toward a more aggressive tightening stance after a period of easing that was intended to cushion a weakening labor market. If the Fed follows Waller’s recommendation, short‑term Treasury yields are likely to climb, compressing the spread between 2‑year and 10‑year notes and prompting a re‑pricing of risk assets. Equity markets could experience heightened volatility, especially in rate‑sensitive sectors such as real estate and utilities, as investors adjust discount rates.

From a policy‑credibility standpoint, a larger move would reinforce the Fed’s commitment to its 2 % inflation target, reducing the risk of an “inflationary mindset” taking hold among businesses and consumers. However, it also raises the specter of tighter financial conditions that could dampen investment and slow the modest growth momentum that has persisted since mid‑2025. The divergent views within the Fed hierarchy suggest that the final decision will balance the need for decisive action against the desire to avoid over‑tightening the economy.

Internationally, a more forceful U.S. rate hike tends to strengthen the dollar, pressuring emerging‑market currencies and potentially fueling capital outflows from those economies. Commodity prices, especially oil, could feel downward pressure as a stronger dollar makes them more expensive in foreign currencies, which may help to ease the energy‑driven component of U.S. inflation.

## FAQ
### What does a 0.5 percentage‑point hike mean for borrowers?
It would raise the cost of new mortgages, auto loans and credit‑card debt, translating into higher monthly payments and potentially slowing credit‑demand growth.

### How might this affect the Fed’s balance sheet plans?
A larger rate increase does not directly alter the quantitative‑tightening schedule, but higher rates can accelerate the runoff of Treasury and agency‑MBS holdings as market participants demand higher yields.

### Could the Fed still opt for a smaller move and rely on forward guidance?
Yes, the alternative path Waller mentioned—a 25‑basis‑point hike with a clear signal for a January increase—remains on the table, but it would require consistent communication to keep markets anchored.

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) released a cost estimate for S. 2801, the Canterbury Shaker Village National Heritage Area Study Authorization Act, as ordered reported by the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources on July 29, 2026. The bill would direct the Secretary of the Interior to conduct a study on the suitability and feasibility of designating the Canterbury Shaker Village in New Hampshire as a National Heritage Area. The CBO projects the legislation would cost approximately $1 million over the 2026-2030 period, a negligible sum relative to the federal budget.

## Key Facts

- **Legislative vehicle**: S. 2801, ordered reported by the Senate Committee on Energy and Natural Resources on July 29, 2026.
- **Primary action**: Directs the Secretary of the Interior to study the suitability and feasibility of designating the Canterbury Shaker Village as a National Heritage Area.
- **Budgetary impact**: Estimated at $1 million over five years (2026–2030).
- **Spending nature**: Discretionary spending, subject to annual appropriations.
- **No new mandates**: The bill contains no intergovernmental or private-sector mandates as defined in the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act (UMRA).
- **No revenue effects**: The CBO estimates no changes in federal revenues.

## Analysis

At first glance, a $1 million authorization for a feasibility study seems trivial—and it is, in the context of a federal budget measured in trillions. However, this cost estimate offers a window into how Washington evaluates even the smallest spending proposals. The CBO’s role is not to judge the merits of a heritage area but to score the fiscal consequences. Here, the score is minimal, meaning the bill is unlikely to face fiscal objections.

National Heritage Areas are a unique policy tool. They are not national parks; they are locally managed entities that receive limited federal technical assistance and modest grants. Designation often unlocks private and local matching funds, which can amplify the initial federal outlay. For the Canterbury Shaker Village, a site of significant cultural and historical value, a heritage area designation could boost tourism and preservation efforts. Yet the economic impact would be highly localized.

From a macro perspective, this bill is a rounding error. It does not move the needle on deficits, inflation, or employment. But it illustrates the fragmented nature of federal spending: thousands of small authorizations that collectively add up. The CBO’s estimate is a reminder that even minor bills undergo rigorous fiscal scrutiny, and that discretionary spending remains subject to the appropriations process.

## Implications

For markets and investors, this bill is essentially irrelevant. There is no tradable signal here. For policy watchers, it is a case study in how Congress allocates small sums for cultural and historical projects. If the study concludes favorably, future legislation could authorize actual designation and funding, but that would be a separate, likely still small, appropriation. The broader takeaway is that fiscal hawks may target even these tiny expenditures as part of a larger effort to trim discretionary spending, though the savings would be negligible.

## FAQ

### What is a National Heritage Area?
A National Heritage Area is a region designated by Congress where natural, cultural, and historic resources combine to form a cohesive landscape. They are managed by local entities and receive limited federal support.

### How much would S. 2801 cost?
The CBO estimates the bill would cost about $1 million over five years, primarily for the feasibility study.

### Does the bill create new mandates?
No. The CBO reports that S. 2801 contains no intergovernmental or private-sector mandates as defined by UMRA.

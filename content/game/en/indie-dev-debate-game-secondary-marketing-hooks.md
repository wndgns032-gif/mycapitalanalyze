## What Happened

A post on an overseas game community by a developer with a decade of experience—spanning AAA studios to solo projects—has ignited a heated debate after he claimed that the game itself is now almost secondary. The developer, posting as Plastic_Study4687, said a conversation with a friend on the publishing side shook him: "The game itself is almost secondary now. The only thing that matters is that the hook is established before a single line of code is written." According to this modern publishing narrative, making an indie game is "easy"; the real challenge is selling it: validate the trailer, screenshots, and key art first, build wishlists, and only then start making the actual product.

The post drew hundreds of comments. The core question: should developers listen to the market or to themselves?

## Why It Matters

The thread exposes a widening rift between two camps. The marketing camp argues that without marketing, you're just playing for yourself. The creative camp counters that chasing data alone produces soulless garbage. Many responders pushed back on the original post. User Sketch0z said, "This isn't new. This has been the path to publisher investment for the last decade." Another noted that the barrier to making games is lower than ever: free engines, Blender, and $100 to get on Steam. "Otherwise why do release numbers break records every year? Because it's hard? Nothing stops you from making a passion project, but if it's your career, treat it like one."

Some questioned the poster's credentials. SWATJester, who worked at multiple AAA studios, said, "I highly doubt anyone in analytics or data science at a publisher would say that. It reads like someone outside the industry, or an AI hallucinating what it thinks AAA development is."

Senior designer MeaningfulChoices offered a middle path: "I don't think 'make a great game and sales will come' was ever true—that's naivety, not business strategy. But I won't swing to the other extreme." He advises building a small vertical slice to validate, then investing big. "Most successful games achieve both art and commerce. They're not mutually exclusive."

Others pointed out that blaming marketing for a game's failure is self-deception. "I've seen countless bad indie games with the same familiar question: 'Marketing didn't work, what did I do wrong?' The game, sir. You did the game wrong." They argued that good games have a communication problem, while bad games are nearly impossible to market, and that "validate the trailer first" is a worldview for people with no concept of game design.

## The Bigger Picture

Even those in marketing offered counterpoints. User unit187 said, "My buddy works at a studio that markets before development. Trailers exploded, games got 150k to over a million wishlists, and then every game they made barely broke even due to poor sales."

User random_boss reframed the hook: it doesn't have to mean "will this make money," but "will people find this interesting enough to rank above their 2 million other distractions." He added, "Success in creative fields is structurally stacked against the individual. It's a lottery ticket that costs years of your life. Someone wins big, which convinces the rest of us to keep buying tickets, and we keep losing." His solution: make games for the love of the process and accept that commercial success likely isn't the real outcome. "You can enjoy making music without assuming you'll become Billie Eilish."

User halfwhitefullblack echoed this: "Since we know the way to make money in this field is to make very boring, soulless stuff, go make money elsewhere and then do this because you want to. Even if only one person plays my game, or no one, that's fine, because I did it for the love of the art."

Data-minded user Idiberug laid out a sobering scenario: assume 7,000 wishlists (the old Steam "magic number"), fewer than 1,000 purchases at launch, plus 3,000 later at a discount, netting $10,000—decent for a first game. Then what? 2,000 install, 1,500 beat the first level, 500 finish, 400 refund, 100 review, peak concurrent 50, and three months later only 200 occasionally open it, with 20 active on Discord. "You succeeded. You earned the price of a used car. Yet a quarter later, at any moment, only a handful of people are playing your game." He cited examples: Loot Loop sold 100,000 copies but had 36 concurrent players four months post-launch; A Game About Feeding A Black Hole sold 200,000 but couldn't break 100 concurrent; and the well-known BRAZILIAN DRUG DEALER 3 now has 22 players online.

## What To Watch Next

The debate is unlikely to be resolved soon. As the article concludes, the real question is whether data validation is the new normal and whether it will stifle personal passion projects. The community already has an answer from one hobbyist: "My first game was made for myself, and so were my second through seventh." Whether publishers will shift their approach, or whether more developers will abandon the traditional path altogether, remains to be seen.

## FAQ

### Is this a new phenomenon?
No. Several commenters noted that validating a game's marketability before full production has been the standard path to publisher investment for roughly the past decade.

### Do data-driven games always succeed?
Not necessarily. One user shared that a studio focused on marketing first achieved huge wishlist numbers but every game barely broke even due to poor sales.

### Can a game succeed without marketing?
The consensus is that great games still need to be discovered, but marketing a bad game is nearly impossible. Most successful titles balance both art and commerce.

### What do developers advise for passion projects?
Many suggest making games for the love of the process while accepting that commercial success is not guaranteed, or funding your passion through other work.

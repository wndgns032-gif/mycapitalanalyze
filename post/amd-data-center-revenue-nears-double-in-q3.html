AMD Data Center Revenue Nears Double in Q3 as AI Chip Demand Accelerates

AMD’s third‑quarter results show a 45% revenue surge and almost a 100% jump in data‑center sales, signalling a rapid expansion of AI‑related computing demand.

Source: CNBC Economy

AMD posted a striking third‑quarter performance, with total revenue climbing 45% year‑over‑year to $13.38 billion and its data‑center segment almost doubling its sales compared with the same period last year. The company beat consensus earnings per share expectations, reporting $2.09 versus the $1.95 forecast, and highlighted a strategic shift toward AI‑driven workloads. While the stock rallied on the earnings beat, it later faced pressure amid broader market worries about excessive AI‑infrastructure spending.

Key Facts

  • Revenue: $13.38 billion in Q3 2026, up from $9.25 billion a year earlier.
  • Data‑center sales: Roughly 100% increase YoY, now representing about 64% of AMD’s total revenue (up from 47%).
  • Earnings per share: Adjusted $2.09, surpassing the $1.95 consensus.
  • Guidance: CEO Lisa Su expects server revenue to double in 2027 and AI‑related data‑center growth to outpace the company’s own three‑month‑old forecast.
  • Supply chain: AMD says it has secured additional wafer, substrate, packaging and memory capacity for 2027.
  • Competitive landscape: Intel reported a 78% rise in its data‑center segment but saw its share price tumble on weak forward guidance.
  • Related deals: AMD committed $1 billion to Meta’s equity raise and up to $5 billion to Anthropic, while also promoting its Helios rack‑scale AI system.

    • Analysis

    The surge in AMD’s data‑center revenue underscores a broader re‑allocation of capital toward AI compute. By capturing a larger share of the server‑CPU market, AMD is positioning itself as a viable alternative to Nvidia’s GPU‑centric AI stack, especially for “agentic” AI applications that rely heavily on CPU‑intensive inference workloads. The company’s claim that data‑center products will drive the bulk of its 2027 growth suggests a pivot away from the declining PC segment, where combined PC‑and‑gaming sales fell 8% YoY.

    From a macro perspective, the rapid scaling of AI‑focused hardware could amplify demand for advanced semiconductor manufacturing capacity, potentially tightening the supply of high‑performance wafers and memory chips. AMD’s proactive engagement with TSMC, SK Hynix and Samsung indicates an effort to lock in capacity before the anticipated 2027 surge, a move that may pressure component pricing and influence inventory strategies across the tech supply chain.

    Investors should weigh the upside of accelerated top‑line growth against the risk that the AI‑spending cycle could cool if corporate budgets tighten amid higher financing costs. The recent pull‑back in AI‑related equities reflects that concern, and AMD’s stock may experience heightened volatility as market sentiment oscillates between optimism over AI adoption and caution about over‑investment.

    Policy‑makers monitoring the AI boom might consider the implications for trade balances and export controls, as high‑value semiconductor equipment and designs become increasingly strategic. A sustained expansion in U.S. AI hardware production could improve the trade deficit in high‑tech goods but also attract regulatory scrutiny.

    FAQ

    How does AMD’s data‑center growth compare with Intel’s?

    AMD’s data‑center revenue almost doubled YoY, while Intel reported a 78% increase; however, Intel’s stock fell sharply on weaker guidance, whereas AMD’s outlook remains bullish.

    What does the shift toward AI‑centric servers mean for the broader PC market?

    The PC segment continues to contract, with AMD’s combined PC‑and‑gaming revenue down 8% YoY, indicating that capital is moving from consumer devices to enterprise AI workloads.

    Should investors expect AMD’s stock to keep rising this year?

    The stock has surged over 120% YTD, but recent pull‑backs tied to AI‑spending concerns suggest that future performance will hinge on how quickly AI projects translate into sustained server demand and whether supply‑chain constraints are managed effectively.

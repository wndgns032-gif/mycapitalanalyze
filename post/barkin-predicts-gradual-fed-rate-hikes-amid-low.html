Barkin Predicts Gradual Fed Rate Hikes Amid Low Unemployment and Sticky Inflation

Richmond Fed President Thomas Barkin says the Federal Reserve will likely raise rates again but at a measured pace due to lingering inflation and near‑record job market strength.

Source: CNBC Finance

Thomas Barkin, the Richmond Fed chief, indicated that the Federal Reserve will probably need to lift rates again, but he argued that a step‑by‑step pace is more appropriate given lingering uncertainty. In a CNBC interview at the 2026 CFO Summit, Barkin tied the combination of a labor market near historic lows with inflation still above the 2 % goal to a “straight‑forward” policy trajectory, while stressing that the timing of moves should remain cautious.

Key Facts

  • The unemployment rate is hovering near its lowest levels in recent decades.
  • Consumer‑price inflation remains above the Fed’s 2 % target, keeping price stability a top concern.
  • The FOMC’s latest decision left the federal‑funds target corridor at 3.75 %‑4.00 %.
  • A 0.25 % increase was approved in September, and most officials expect at least one more hike before the calendar year ends.
  • Barkin, not a voting FOMC member until 2027, described current policy as roughly neutral, perhaps slightly accommodative.
  • CME’s FedWatch tool shows a >80 % probability of a December rate hike and a further adjustment in 2027.
  • He cited artificial‑intelligence‑related cost pressures as a short‑run inflation driver, but expects the technology to eventually raise productive capacity and dampen price growth.

    • Analysis

    Barkin’s remarks reinforce the market’s expectation that the Fed will not pause its tightening cycle outright. Treasury yields have already inched higher since the interview, with the 10‑year note climbing a few basis points as investors price in a modestly higher terminal rate. Equity markets, particularly rate‑sensitive sectors such as utilities and real‑estate investment trusts, have shown renewed volatility, reflecting the tension between solid job growth and stubborn price pressures.

    From a portfolio‑construction standpoint, the signal of a “gradual” path favours strategies that position for incremental yield gains rather than a sharp spike. Fixed‑income managers may extend duration modestly, betting that the Fed’s tightening will be paced enough to avoid a steep curve inversion. Conversely, growth‑oriented equity investors might tilt toward companies with pricing power and strong balance sheets, as these firms are better equipped to navigate a higher‑cost environment.

    Implications

    For policymakers, Barkin’s stance underscores the difficulty of balancing a neutral stance with the need to curb inflation. By labeling policy as “slightly accommodative,” he signals that the Fed is willing to accept a small amount of stimulus while still moving upward. This could translate into a series of 25‑basis‑point hikes spread over the next six months, rather than a single aggressive move.

    Investors should monitor upcoming data releases—especially the monthly CPI report and the weekly initial‑jobless‑claims figures—as they will provide the “information” Barkin referenced. A surprise slowdown in inflation could accelerate the pace of hikes, while a sudden labor‑market wobble might prompt a more dovish turn.

    Internationally, a steadier U.S. rate‑rise trajectory may reduce the likelihood of abrupt capital‑flow shocks to emerging markets, but the continued prospect of higher U.S. yields will keep pressure on foreign‑currency debt and could sustain a modest dollar strength.

    FAQ

    What does “gradual” mean for the timing of the next Fed hike?

    Barkin’s language suggests the Fed may add 25 basis points every two to three meetings, rather than delivering a large move in a single session.

    How should bond investors react to a potentially higher terminal rate?

    A measured hike path usually supports a gentle steepening of the yield curve; investors might favour intermediate‑term bonds that benefit from higher yields without the volatility of short‑term rates.

    Will AI‑driven price pressures change the inflation outlook?

    In the short run, AI‑related supply chain disruptions can add cost pressure, but over the longer horizon the technology is expected to boost productivity and help bring inflation back toward target.

Bank of England FPC Warns Interconnected Risks Are Converging

The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee says sovereign yields, AI-related leverage and private credit strains could crystallise together, while holding the UK countercyclical buffer at 2%.

Source: Bank of England

The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee used its September 2026 record to deliver a blunt message: the risks it tracks are no longer separate problems but a single, tightly linked cluster that could unravel at once. The committee kept its main crisis-fighting tool unchanged, but the tone of the assessment shifted meaningfully.

Key Facts

The FPC said the risk outlook has deteriorated since July, with interconnected vulnerabilities now more likely to crystallise simultaneously. Re-escalation of conflict in the Middle East has renewed uncertainty over growth and rate paths, intensifying the chance that sovereign debt, stretched asset valuations and risky credit all crack together.

Sovereign yields across several advanced economies have climbed to levels unseen since 2008, tightening global financial conditions. Markets have adjusted gradually and the system has stayed resilient, but hedge fund leverage in gilts, though stable, remains elevated.

Equity markets have broadly absorbed higher yields, yet AI-linked valuations fell sharply in July as stretched positions unwound. Debt financing of AI investment keeps growing fast, with 2026 global issuance expected to exceed that of countries such as the UK, raising concerns about opacity and circular financing arrangements.

Frontier AI incidents, where autonomous models acted unexpectedly, reinforced warnings about cyber and operational risk. Domestically, the FPC judged households, corporates and banks resilient, with GDP holding up better than expected. It kept the countercyclical capital buffer at 2% and confirmed it will proceed with leverage ratio reforms, with consultation expected in early 2027.

Analysis

The striking element is the FPC's framing of correlation. A supply shock from energy prices feeds higher sovereign yields, which pressure valuations, which stress leveraged positions, which then test credit markets. Each link is manageable alone; together they form a feedback loop.

The AI angle is genuinely new. When a single sector's capital expenditure becomes systemically important, its financing structure matters as much as its earnings. Heavy debt issuance, limited disclosure and circular funding arrangements make losses harder to map, so a disappointment in productivity expectations could hit both AI assets and the sovereign bonds underpinning them.

The gilt market remains the soft spot. Elevated hedge fund leverage in a core sterling market means a sharp repricing is possible even without a fresh shock. That explains the emphasis on repo market resilience over blanket capital increases.

Implications

For investors, the message is that diversification across asset classes may offer less protection than assumed if a common shock drives yields, equities and credit together. Duration risk and leveraged carry trades look especially exposed. AI-themed credit deserves scrutiny of structure, not just headline growth.

For policy, the FPC is choosing targeted tools over broad buffers. Holding the CCyB at 2% preserves lending capacity, while leverage ratio reforms could free balance sheet room, some of which may flow into prime brokerage and gilt leverage. That is a deliberate trade-off: more market liquidity against more concentrated risk, which is why the committee flagged a possible future increase in the general leverage buffer above 25 basis points.

FAQ

Why did the FPC keep the countercyclical buffer at 2%?

A neutral setting keeps banks able to absorb shocks without cutting lending unnecessarily. The committee sees household and corporate balance sheets as broadly stable, so it opted for targeted measures instead of a broad capital increase.

What makes AI-related debt a financial stability concern?

Rapid issuance, limited transparency and circular financing make it hard to assess true exposure. If productivity expectations disappoint, losses could spread across funding markets and even sovereign debt, not just AI equities.

Why focus on the gilt repo market?

Hedge fund leverage there is stable but high. A disorderly unwind could disrupt core sterling markets, so the FPC favours market-based reforms to improve repo resilience rather than relying only on bank capital rules.

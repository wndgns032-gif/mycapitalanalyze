ECB Supervisory Risk Appetite: Why Elderson Wants Fewer, Sharper Bank Checks

The ECB is raising its supervisory risk tolerance and simplifying bank oversight, trading exhaustive annual reviews for sharper prioritisation and faster remediation.

Source: ECB

The ECB's top banking supervisor wants Europe's watchdogs to stop trying to see everything at once. In remarks delivered in Bali, Frank Elderson argued that a more fragmented, technologically disrupted and climate-exposed financial system demands sharper prioritisation rather than broader coverage.

Key Facts

Elderson, Vice-Chair of the ECB's Supervisory Board, describes a risk landscape that is not merely larger but more uncertain, interconnected and volatile. Banks now contend with geopolitical fragmentation, rapid technological change, swinging energy and commodity prices, rising inflation, demographic shifts, deeper ties to non-bank financial institutions, and persistent climate and nature-related risks.

His response rests on three pillars. First, a formal risk tolerance framework (RTF) that explicitly states how much residual supervisory risk is acceptable when certain areas are reviewed less intensively or deferred. Deprioritisation, he stresses, is an active judgment, not a passive by-product of limited resources. Second, simplification: over 100 supervisory guidance publications reviewed, roughly 40 discontinued, securitisation approvals cut from three months to about seven days, stress-test data points reduced by around 55%, and capital-related approval timelines shortened from several months to under six days. Third, timely remediation through escalation, with time-bound, root-cause-oriented fixes and willingness to use enforcement powers.

He cites the 2023 banking turmoil as proof that meeting minimum capital and liquidity ratios can coexist with accumulating weaknesses in governance, risk culture and business models.

Analysis

This is a notable shift in supervisory philosophy. The traditional instinct after a crisis is to add checks, layers and reporting requirements. Elderson proposes the opposite: fewer, better-targeted interventions, backed by a willingness to escalate when problems persist.

The logic is defensible. Supervision is a scarce resource. If every risk at every bank is examined annually with equal intensity, attention gets spread thinly and genuinely material vulnerabilities may go unnoticed. A framework that openly accepts residual risk in low-priority areas frees capacity for the exposures that could actually threaten solvency or liquidity.

There is a tension worth flagging. Raising risk tolerance works only if the prioritisation itself is sound. If supervisors misjudge which risks are material, the framework becomes a mechanism for legitimising blind spots rather than managing them. The 2023 failures showed that the most damaging weaknesses were often hiding in plain sight.

Elderson also places responsibility on banks, arguing they should stop seeking ever-higher legal certainty through endless guidance requests. That is a cultural argument as much as a regulatory one, and it echoes the European Commission's recent competitiveness report.

Implications

For banks, the practical effect is a lighter procedural burden in standardised, lower-risk activities and faster approvals, but less comfort from prescriptive rules. More will depend on supervisory judgment, which cuts both ways: less box-ticking, but also less predictability.

For investors, the signal is that European supervision is becoming more judgment-driven. Institutions with strong governance and clear risk cultures may find the new regime easier to navigate. Those relying on formal compliance alone could face sharper escalation if underlying weaknesses persist.

For policymakers, the experiment matters beyond Europe. If prioritisation delivers the same safety with less friction, it becomes a template. If it misses a crisis, the pendulum swings back toward exhaustive inspection.

FAQ

What is the ECB's risk tolerance framework?

It is a formal tool that defines how much residual supervisory risk the ECB accepts when certain areas at individual banks are reviewed less intensively or deferred to a later date, making deprioritisation an explicit decision rather than an oversight.

Has simplification lowered safety standards?

Elderson insists it has not. The stated aim is to cut unnecessary complexity and free capacity for material risks while preserving essential safeguards, with faster approvals for lower-risk transactions such as standardised securitisations.

Why does remediation matter so much?

Because identifying a weakness is worthless if it is not fixed. The 2023 turmoil showed that unresolved known problems can become costly, so the ECB wants time-bound fixes targeting root causes and escalation to enforcement when needed.

The Yield Curve Un-Inversion: Recession Signal or False Alarm?

The 2s10s curve has steepened back into positive territory. History says the clock starts now — but this cycle may be different.

The most reliable recession indicator of the past half-century just sent a message that nobody wants to hear. The yield curve — the spread between 2-year and 10-year Treasury yields — has moved back into positive territory after a prolonged inversion. Historically, that transition, known as "un-inversion," has marked the start of the recession countdown rather than the all-clear.

Why the Inversion Happened

The 2s10s curve inverted when the Federal Reserve raised short-term rates aggressively while long-term rates stayed anchored by falling inflation expectations and a slowing growth outlook. An inverted curve means investors expect rates to fall in the future — typically because they expect the economy to weaken enough to force the Fed's hand.

What made this inversion unusual was its duration. The curve stayed inverted longer than in any prior cycle, driven by the speed of the tightening and the unusual resilience of the labor market. That resilience kept recession at bay far longer than the curve's historical track record suggested it should.

Un-Inversion and the Recession Clock

The classic pattern is: curve inverts, stays inverted, then un-inverts as the Fed begins cutting and long rates fall more slowly than short rates. Recession typically follows the un-inversion with a lag of roughly six to eighteen months. The logic is that by the time the Fed is cutting, the damage has already been done to credit and demand.

If this cycle follows the historical template, the un-inversion we are now witnessing would place a recession somewhere in 2027. But this cycle could be different. The inversion was caused as much by a supply shock — the post-pandemic inflation surge — as by traditional overheating, and the corporate sector entered the cycle with unusually strong balance sheets.

What to Watch

The decisive variables are credit spreads and the labor market. If spreads remain tight and unemployment stays low, the soft-landing scenario survives. If spreads begin to widen and layoffs accelerate, the curve's reputation will be vindicated once again. The curve tells you the direction of risk, not the precise date — and right now, it is pointing toward caution.

HSBC CEO Cautions on Data Center Financing as AI Debt Boom Accelerates

HSBC's Georges Elhedery says AI is transformational but warns investors to scrutinize data center tenant quality and contract pricing as hyperscaler borrowing reshapes credit markets.

Source: CNBC Finance

The head of Europe's largest bank has endorsed artificial intelligence as a genuine economic force while cautioning that the infrastructure financing boom surrounding it is not uniformly sound. His remarks land at a moment when debt markets, government borrowing and corporate capital spending are increasingly intertwined.

Key Facts

HSBC chief executive Georges Elhedery told CNBC that AI will be "transformational," boosting employee productivity and helping the bank re-engineer its operations. On the investment side, however, he urged selectivity: lenders should assess the quality of tenancy agreements and the pricing embedded in data center contracts, since some deals are sturdy while others look considerably weaker.

HSBC, which holds close to $3 trillion in assets and has flagged ambitions to serve fast-growing transition ecosystems including data centers, both finances and operates such facilities.

McKinsey estimates global data center spending could approach $7 trillion by 2030. Big Tech is increasingly turning to private credit and bond markets to fund construction. Mohamed El-Erian of Allianz noted that hyperscalers raised roughly $200 billion in bonds last year and may raise around $1 trillion this year, describing them as insensitive to yields and spreads because they are betting on a transformational technology. JPMorgan Chase projects $4.1 trillion in AI-related debt issuance by decade's end. Sovereign yields in several major economies have reached multi-decade highs in 2026 amid heavy deficits, refinancing at higher rates, sticky inflation and hawkish central banks.

Analysis

Elhedery's distinction between the technology and the assets built to host it is the crux. AI adoption can raise productivity across an economy even if individual data center projects disappoint. Infrastructure lending, by contrast, is only as good as the cash flows behind it, which depend on tenant creditworthiness and contract duration.

The macro backdrop sharpens this. Governments are absorbing a large share of global savings to refinance debt at elevated rates, while hyperscalers issue record volumes of bonds. Because these borrowers are pursuing strategic scale rather than spread, they can crowd out more price-sensitive issuers, keeping borrowing costs high for everyone else.

That creates a two-tier market. Investment-grade hyperscaler paper finds buyers easily; smaller operators and leveraged developers may struggle, and lenders extending credit against speculative capacity could face refinancing pressure if demand or pricing softens. A banker publicly flagging tenant quality is a signal that underwriting standards, not enthusiasm, will determine who survives the cycle.

Implications

For investors, the message favors selectivity: exposure to AI via cash-rich platform companies differs fundamentally from exposure via leveraged infrastructure. Credit investors should watch issuance calendars, spread dispersion and lease terms. For policy, heavy corporate and sovereign issuance together complicates the inflation outlook and limits how quickly central banks can ease. For banks, data center lending is attractive but demands rigorous diligence — a point that may slow marginal projects and reward operators with strong tenants.

FAQ

Why does tenant quality matter so much in data center lending?

Because loan repayment depends on long-term lease cash flows. If a tenant is weakly capitalized or contracts are short and repriced downward, the asset's value and the lender's recovery prospects deteriorate quickly.

Are hyperscaler bonds a threat to other borrowers?

They add substantial supply to credit markets. Since these issuers are relatively insensitive to spreads, they can absorb demand that might otherwise fund more rate-sensitive companies, keeping financing costs elevated.

Does this mean the AI investment theme is overhyped?

Not necessarily. The technology may still transform productivity; the caution concerns financing structures and asset-level economics, where returns will vary widely across projects and operators.
